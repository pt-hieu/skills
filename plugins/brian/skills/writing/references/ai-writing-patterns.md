# AI writing patterns to audit against

Reference for auditing prose a human reads against the patterns that mark it as machine-made. Part 1 is a checklist ordered by how strongly each pattern marks prose as machine-made. Part 2 lists sources. Part 3 is a word-level replacement table, with the words that mark Claude's current register ranked by evidence.

A single hit proves little. Readers who use LLMs daily spot machine prose at about 90% accuracy, and they do it from density: several tells in one passage. Wikipedia's guidance page keeps a list of signs that prove nothing on their own (perfect grammar, formal register, transition words), and Graphite's 2026 study found most word-level tells change between model versions. Structural tells are the stable ones.

## Part 1: checklist

### Narrative shape (strongest, and the level where Claude-drafted essays fail)

1. **Even-paced log.** One dated clause per event, same distance from every event, no slowing for the one that mattered and no skipping of the dull stretch. Human telling spends three paragraphs in scene on one bad afternoon and one clause on three weeks. Measured by Tian et al. (EMNLP 2024) and Wang et al. (2023).
2. **Forward-only, single-track chronology.** Events in the order they happened, every paragraph serving the main line. Human essays open at the crisis and loop back, jump ahead ("I didn't know yet that..."), and carry a tangent. StoryScope (2026): subplots absent in 79% of AI stories against 57% of human ones.
3. **Hindsight-only narration.** Confusion is summarised ("after some debugging I realised") and never lived on the page. Human telling holds the reader inside the wrong belief for a while. Whittet (Chronicle, 2025): "structure without struggle."
4. **Flat-observer narrator.** Things happen and the narrator "found", "noticed", "realised". Human narrators act, err, and show irritation or delight as it happens. Jiang and Hyland (2025) measured far fewer engagement markers in ChatGPT essays.
5. **Morally clean narrator.** Every choice was reasonable or has already been learned from. Human narrators stay implicated: still unsure, still a bit ashamed. StoryScope: ambivalent protagonist in 38% of AI stories against 59% of human ones.
6. **Stepping around the worst moment.** The most painful event gets one line and the essay hurries to recovery. Human telling slows down there and stays. Livingstone (2026) on the AI-assisted Modern Love essay.
7. **Climax early, flat run-out.** The big turn arrives midway and later sections explain it. Human arcs keep rising toward the end. Tian et al. 2024.
8. **Clean causal chain.** Every event has one tidy cause, "X led to Y led to Z". Human accounts give several causes, luck, other people's choices, and some outcomes left unexplained.
9. **Stated theme and a moral at every section end.** StoryScope: narrator states the theme in 77% of AI stories against 52% of human ones. Human sections end on an image, a fact, or an unresolved beat.
10. **Fractal summaries and recap ending.** Intro previews the lessons, each section restates one, the close lists them. Popular AI (2026) calls the wrap-up "the strongest single tell" and advises leaving "one button undone".
11. **Earned ending.** The last line turns loss into growth, gratitude, or acceptance. StoryScope: acceptance-resolutions in 47% of AI stories against 27%. Human endings let a loss stay a loss.
12. **Inflated significance.** A small event becomes a turning point, a legacy, or a broader trend. Wikipedia: "Undue emphasis on significance, legacy, and broader trends."
13. **One point restated in every section.** Nothing develops; the thesis returns with new imagery. tropes.fyi "one-point dilution"; Michel (2025) on the missing "idea arc".
14. **Engineered callbacks.** An early phrase returns at a section end as a payoff ("those are the same habits that produced X"). Human callbacks are rare and slightly off. tropes.fyi "self-echo", "the tie-back".
15. **Aphoristic or reversal headings.** "The X was never the problem." Each heading previews its section's lesson. Plain labels ("The hangs", "June") are the human norm. Inferred from Whittet's "punchy inversions" and tropes.fyi; no source catalogues headings directly.
16. **Template architecture with evenly weighted sections.** Background, problem, attempt, insight, lessons, conclusion, each the same length. Popular AI 2026.
17. **Generic or arbitrary detail.** Either placeholders that fit anyone ("late nights", "a tough conversation") or concrete detail chosen for vividness that carries no point of view. Chakrabarty et al. (CHI 2025) list "lack of specificity" among seven categories from professional writers' edits; Michel: "why marigolds?"
18. **Numbers and dates as texture.** Figures that give verisimilitude and never change what the reader understands. A number earns its place when it moves a decision. Inferred, sibling of 17.
19. **Own logs and commit messages as punchlines.** A deadpan log line set off to close a section. Inferred; combines staged fragments, reveal, and section moral.
20. **Vague allusion instead of a named source.** "The classic advice", "experts say". StoryScope: vague allusions in 72% of AI stories against 50%.
21. **Dialogue that delivers the thesis.** A remembered line of speech exists to state the essay's idea. StoryScope: 59% against 34%.

### Rhythm and architecture

22. **Metronomic paragraphs.** Same length, same energy, each resolving on its last sentence. Popular AI: "real prose has dents."
23. **Short punchy fragments for staged emphasis.** "Not a detail. A design decision." explainx (2026) calls this the most common Claude tic and the least noticed.
24. **Uniform sentence length.** Low burstiness. Graphite: AI sentence lengths vary far less than human ones.
25. **Anaphora.** Successive sentences or paragraphs open the same way. tropes.fyi.
26. **Announce-then-deliver preambles.** "Two things made this hard." tropes.fyi.
27. **Answering objections nobody raised.** Defending a minor choice, reassuring against a misreading. tropes.fyi "belaboring the unnecessary".
28. **Reveal by rhetorical question or colon.** "The culprit? A missing index." Shelby (SEJ, 2025); tropes.fyi.
29. **Summary sentence closing each paragraph.** Wikipedia "section summaries", citing Ju et al. (ACL 2025).
30. **Point and counterpoint in every paragraph.** Robbins: "unnaturally perfect balance."

### Sentence level

31. **Negative parallelism.** "Not X but Y", "it's not just X, it's Y", "less like an X and more like a Y", "no X, no Y, just Z", "Y rather than X", and the cross-sentence form "This is not X. Rather, it is Y." The Atlantic (2026) calls this the most reliable current tell, ahead of em-dashes and "delve". Graphite: corrective framing runs at about 12 times the human rate and rising. The writing skill covers it under "Positive definition".
32. **Rule of three.** Three adjectives, three supports, three examples. Strongest where a human would never bother. Wikipedia; Russell et al. (ACL 2025).
33. **Trailing participial analysis clause.** "..., highlighting the strong ties", "..., ensuring reliability". Reinhart et al. (PNAS 2025): the strongest grammatical signal, 2 to 5 times the human rate.
34. **Copula avoidance.** "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features" for "is" and "has". Wikipedia; Geng and Trotta.
35. **Flagging importance.** "This distinction matters because", "what matters more than X is Y". Graphite: "distinction matters" has zero occurrences in the human corpus.
36. **Tradeoff-free promises.** "without sacrificing", "without losing", "without requiring". Graphite, strong for 2026 models.
37. **Inanimate subjects doing the analysis.** "The data reveals", "the folktale subverts". The human analyst vanishes. Belcher (2025).
38. **Pseudo-cleft frames.** "What makes this work is...", "The thing most people miss is...". Reinhart: that-clause subjects at 2.6 times the human rate.
39. **Em-dash as contrast hinge.** Reliable for 2023 to 2025 text. Newer models are trained away from it, and Graphite finds Claude Opus 5 at about the human rate, so count it only when it carries a negative parallelism.
40. **Elegant variation.** Cycling synonyms for one referent ("says", then "notes", then "explains"). Wikipedia; Belcher.
41. **Mannered sensory metaphor.** "Sorrow tastes of metal", "a liminal day". Kriss (NYT, 2025); Graphite scores Claude at 2.5 times the human rate.
42. **Emotion through stock bodily sensation.** Tight chest, stomach drop, cold sweat. StoryScope: 81% of AI stories against 38%; plain emotion labels ("I was bored") are the human marker.

### Word level (era-specific; weak alone)

43. **Current overused vocabulary.** 2023 to 2024: delve, tapestry, testament, intricate, pivotal, landscape, garner, boasts, meticulous. 2024 to 2025: align with, foster, showcase, highlight, enhance, underscore, vibrant. 2025 to 2026: emphasise, dependable, practical, deliberate, steady, measured, genuinely, profound, quietly, multifaceted. Graphite: "is genuinely" appears 1,021 times in Claude Opus 5 articles against 4 in human ones.
44. **Significance puffery.** "marking a pivotal moment", "a testament to", "setting the stage for", "indelible mark".
45. **Hedging and disclaimers.** "it's important to note", "may vary", "based on available information".
46. **Signposting transitions.** "Additionally", "Ultimately", "Looking ahead", "Together these". Weak alone.
47. **Nominalisation and long words.** Reinhart: 1.5 to 2 times the human rate; average word length 5.2 to 5.7 characters against 4.9.

### Human markers worth keeping when they appear

First person acting, direct address to the reader, casual qualifiers ("pretty", "sort of"), parentheses, exclamation marks, superlatives ("the first", "the only"), downtoners ("perhaps", "tends to"), plain verbs (wrote, used, died), minor errors, tangents, one oddly specific detail only this writer could know, a loss left as a loss, and an ending that stops.

## Part 3: word replacements

The rows run strongest evidence first: measured at 100 times the human rate or more, then 10 to 100 times, then under 10 times or measured only against another model, then editors' and community consensus. The most relevant block for Claude-drafted prose is the "PR lift" rows, which come from a corpus of about 505k GitHub pull request texts (louisabraham.github.io/load-bearing, data dated 2026-10-12) and rank the words that mark the Claude-associated cluster against the rest of the corpus. Those are the current Claude engineering register, and they are the words a Claude-edited draft is most likely to carry.

How to read the evidence column:

- "Nx" is Graphite's rate against human articles written before ChatGPT, unless another comparison is named. Graphite's September 2026 study covers Claude Opus 5, GPT-6 Astra, and Gemini 3.1 Pro; its 1 October 2026 update covers Opus 5.5. There is no per-model table for Opus 4.x.
- "GPTZero list" rows: three are confirmed as GPTZero's by Forbes or EdScoop. The rest come from IsGPT's reproduction of a top-50 list that does not name its source. Most of those phrases are academic or fiction stock phrases.
- "PR lift" is the word's lift in the Claude cluster of pull request text, against the rest of the corpus rather than a human baseline.
- "Kobak r" and "Kobak δ" come from Kobak et al. on 2024 PubMed abstracts: r is the excess frequency ratio and δ the excess frequency gap.
- "lpr" is the log prevalence ratio from Juzek et al., "AI-Associated Lexical Shifts Across 34 Languages" (arXiv 2605.25358, May 2026), GPT-4.1 against human news text.
- "WP:AISIGNS / item 43–46" means the word is in Part 1's era lists already.
