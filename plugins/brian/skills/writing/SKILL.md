# Writing

Apply these rules to text you write or rewrite.

To audit a whole draft for machine prose, or when a rule's reason or replacement word is unclear, read [`references/ai-writing-patterns.md`](references/ai-writing-patterns.md). It is the reference these rules distil: a checklist ranked by evidence, its sources, and a word replacement table.

## Rules

**Write the current version.** When Brian corrects a fact in a draft, rewrite the passage as if the corrected fact had been there from the first draft. The reader starts from this version, so cut any sentence that denies, contrasts with, or reassures against an earlier one.

**Positive definition.** State what is true: define a thing by what it is and does. A contrast that defines it by what it is not or cannot do counts whether it is in one sentence ("A, not B", "A, but B") or split across two ("A. It is not B."), because the split keeps the contrast. Exclusion words make the same contrast in fewer words: "only", "just", "nothing else", "on its own", "without", "no longer", and negative indefinites such as "nobody", "nothing", "never", or "no X". Replace them with the event that happens, and let the reader infer the excluded case from the positive claim, or drop it. Use a contrast when the contrast itself is the point the reader needs.

**Answer the questions the reader has reached.** Confirmations and concessions such as "and it does", "it is true, too", "to be fair", or "in fact" argue with an absent skeptic, and so do unrequested justifications: a "because" defending a structure the text already makes clear, a hypothetical about what would go wrong otherwise, a reassurance that a margin or limit is enough, or an endorsement such as "we would do it again". Test each reason clause by deleting it: if the reader loses a fact they need to follow a decision, keep it; if what they lose is a defense, cut it. State the claim once and let it stand. Pre-emptive clarifications count too: a clause that settles a question the text has yet to raise, such as who did something by hand or how a result was judged. Put the fact where the reader first needs it, or drop it. Test each clause by asking what question it answers and whether the reader has reached that question yet.

**One thought, one sentence.** End a sentence where the thought ends. A thought chopped into short sentences reads as staccato and leaves the reader to rebuild the links between them, so write it as one sentence whose grammar holds those links. Use a short sentence for a point meant to land with weight.

**Narrator on the page.** In a first-person piece, the writer is the subject of the sentences: I guessed, I checked, I was wrong, I did this because I wanted that. A tool, a diagnosis, or an audit that "found" something hides what the writer was doing and thinking at the time, so put their action or belief in the sentence and let the reader hold their wrong guess with them until it breaks. Other prose keeps the voice it has.

**Uneven pace.** Spend words where the stakes are and compress the rest. The scene that mattered gets room, a dull stretch gets a clause, and events of unequal weight get unequal space. A date, count, or version number stays where it changes what the reader understands; elsewhere write "a week later" or drop it. A run of "On [date], X happened" sentences is a changelog.

**Sections end on the event.** End a section on the event, the image, or the open question. State a lesson once, in the place where it was learned, and end the other sections on their own material; cut a closing line that draws the lesson, calls back an earlier phrase, or restates what the paragraph showed. Headings name the content ("The hangs"), and an aphorism or reversal as a heading gives the section's ending away.

**Quote for the words.** Quote a log, a brief, or a commit message when the reader needs its words, and say the rest in the writer's own voice. A quote placed at the end of a paragraph to land the point is a punchline.

**Odd counts.** Lists come in the number the material has. A third item added to make three, or a before-and-after pair built for symmetry, reads as pattern.

**Plain words.** Use the word a careful engineer says aloud: "use" for "leverage", "is" for "serves as" or "stands as", "has" for "carries", "means" for what a line of code "says", "cause" for "root cause", "a lot" for "enormously". The words that mark Claude's own register in 2026 are "quietly", "genuinely", "plainly", "deliberate", "load-bearing", "seam", "carries", "sits", "stood", "nobody", "nothing", "survives", "earns its keep", "rests on", "exactly", "in practice", "this matters", "matters because", and code that "argues", "refuses", "tells", or "judges". Keep each of them for its literal sense. The full table with replacements is Part 3 of the reference.

**The ending stops.** A loss stays a loss, an open question stays open, and the last line states a fact and leaves the verdict on the whole to the reader. A close that reframes failure as growth, or that re-teaches the lessons, is the strongest single sign of machine prose.

## Finish

Reread every passage you wrote or rewrote against each rule, applying the rules' own tests: delete each reason clause and see what the reader loses, ask what question each clause answers and whether the reader has reached it, and check every flagged word for its literal sense. Stop when a full pass leaves the text unchanged.
